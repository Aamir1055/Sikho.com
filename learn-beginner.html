What Is a Stock?

A stock (also called a share or equity) is a unit of ownership in a company. If a company divides itself into 1,00,000 shares and you buy 100 of them, you own 0.1% of that business.

Definition
A share represents part-ownership of a company. Shareholders can benefit from the company's growth through a rising share price and dividends.

How the Market Works

Share prices are set by demand and supply, just like anything else you buy. Buyers place "bids" (the price they'll pay) and sellers place "asks" (the price they want). A trade happens when a bid and ask match.

  • More buyers than sellers â†’ the price rises.
  • More sellers than buyers â†’ the price falls.
  • News, results, and emotions constantly shift this balance.
Prices wiggle daily but a growing business trends up over years.
Key takeaway: Price is simply what buyers and sellers agree on right now.

Everything is regulated by SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India), which protects investors and keeps the market fair.

Note
You never trade directly on the exchange â€” you go through a registered broker, who connects you to it.

Demat & Trading Account

To invest you need two linked accounts, opened through a broker:

  • Demat account: stores your shares digitally, like a locker.
  • Trading account: used to place buy and sell orders.
  • Bank account: linked so money can move in and out.

What you need to open one

  1. PAN card
  2. Aadhaar (for KYC and e-sign)
  3. Bank account details
  4. A photo and signature
Tip
Compare brokers on charges (brokerage, annual fees), app quality, and customer support before choosing one.
Key takeaway: Pick a SEBI-registered broker with low fees and an easy app.

Indices: Nifty & Sensex

An index is a basket of major companies used to measure the overall market's direction. When the news says "the market went up 1% today," they usually mean the index.

  • Nifty 50 â€” the 50 largest, most-traded companies on the NSE.

IPOs & the Primary Market

When a private company wants to raise money, it can sell shares to the public for the first time through an IPO (Initial Public Offering). This happens in the primary market. After listing, those shares trade among investors in the secondary market â€” the everyday stock market.

Note
A hyped IPO is not always a good investment. Read the company's prospectus and understand the business first.
Key takeaway: IPO = a company's first sale of shares to the public.

Market Participants

Different people trade for different reasons:

  • Retail investors: individuals like you.
  • Institutions: mutual funds, banks, insurers â€” the "big money."
  • FIIs & DIIs: foreign and domestic institutional investors whose large flows can move the whole market.
Key takeaway: Big institutional buying/selling often drives large market moves.

Bull vs Bear Market

Two words you'll hear constantly:

  • Bull market: prices are generally rising and optimism is high.
  • Bear market: prices are generally falling and fear is high.
Tip
Great long-term investors buy quality companies during bear markets, when others are fearful.

Dividends & Returns

You can earn from shares in two ways:

  • Capital gains: selling a share for more than you paid.
  • Dividends: a share of the company's profit paid to shareholders, usually in cash.
Example
You own 100 shares. The company declares a â‚¹5 dividend per share â†’ you receive â‚¹500, on top of any rise in the share price.
Key takeaway: Total return = price growth + dividends received.

The Power of Compounding & SIP

Compounding means your returns start earning their own returns. Over many years this snowballs into a large amount. A SIP (Systematic Investment Plan) invests a fixed sum every month, so you build wealth steadily without trying to time the market.

Small, regular investments grow steeply over time.

Even â‚¹1,000/month started early can grow into a large corpus over 15â€“20 years thanks to compounding.
Key takeaway: Start early and stay consistent â€” time does the heavy lifting.

Risk & Diversification

Every investment carries risk; the goal is to manage it, not avoid it.

  • Never invest money you'll need soon, or borrowed money.
  • Diversify: spread money across several companies and sectors.
  • Keep an emergency fund separate from your investments.
Warning
Putting all your money into one "hot" stock is the fastest way to get hurt. Spread your risk.
Key takeaway: Don't put all your eggs in one basket.

Taxes & Charges

Two things quietly reduce your returns â€” know them:

  • Brokerage & charges: small fees per trade, plus taxes like STT and GST.
  • Capital gains tax: tax on profits. Short-term (held under a year) is taxed higher than long-term.
Note
Tax rules change over time and vary by situation â€” check current rules or consult a professional before filing.

Smart Habits for Beginners

Good behaviour beats clever tips:

  • Think long term and ignore daily noise.
  • Avoid tips, hype, and "guaranteed return" schemes.
  • Keep learning and maintain a simple record of your trades.
  • Invest regularly, not emotionally.
Key takeaway: Patience and discipline are a beginner's biggest advantages.
